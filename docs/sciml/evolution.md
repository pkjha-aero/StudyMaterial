# How the field evolved

<span class="status status-working">working</span>
<span class="pillar">pillar 9</span>

!!! abstract "In one minute"
    - Scientific ML did not arrive as one idea. It assembled from **four largely independent lines** — reduced-order modelling, data-driven turbulence closure, physics-constrained networks, and operator learning — that only started citing each other around 2019.
    - The through-line is **what gets amortised**. Each generation widened the set of questions one training investment could answer: parameters, then families, then geometries.
    - **Physics entered as bias, not as a loss term.** Karniadakis' framing — observational, inductive and learning biases — is the cleanest way to see what every method on this site is actually doing.
    - The current frontier is **geometry generalisation at industrial scale**: transformer operators on raw point clouds, trained on thousands of CFD solves, deployed as design-loop surrogates.
    - Nothing has been retired. ROMs, PINNs and operators answer different questions, and the newest method is not the right one for most problems.

## The five eras

### Era 1 — Projection (1967 onward): reduce the state

The first idea was that a high-dimensional flow lives near a low-dimensional manifold. **Proper orthogonal decomposition** (Lumley, 1967) extracts an energy-optimal basis from snapshots; Galerkin projection of the governing equations onto that basis gives a small dynamical system.

This is *intrusive* — it needs the operator, not just the data — and it is bounded by a theorem rather than by engineering effort. The **Kolmogorov \(n\)-width** of an advection-dominated problem decays slowly, so no small linear subspace represents a moving front. That barrier is why everything after this era is nonlinear.

### Era 2 — Identification (2010–2016): learn the model, not the state

**Dynamic mode decomposition** (Schmid, 2010) dropped the requirement for the governing operator: fit a best-fit linear operator between consecutive snapshots and read off the modes. It is purely data-driven, which made it immediately usable on experiments.

**SINDy** (Brunton, Proctor & Kutz, 2016) went further and asked for the *equations*. Build a library of candidate terms, then use sparse regression to select the few that matter. The output is a symbolic model you can read, differentiate and trust outside the training data in a way no black box allows — the earliest clear statement that **interpretability is a technical requirement in science, not a preference**.

In parallel, and almost independently, the turbulence community arrived at data-driven modelling from the other direction. **Field inversion and machine learning** (Parish & Duraisamy, 2016) made the key methodological move: do not regress the closure directly against data, because the data does not contain it. Instead, solve an *inverse problem* for the spatial discrepancy field that would have made the model right, then learn a map from local flow features to that discrepancy. **Ling, Kurzawski & Templeton (2016)** contributed the other durable idea of this era — embed the invariance, with a tensor-basis network whose output is Galilean- and rotation-invariant by construction.

### Era 3 — Physics as bias (2017–2019): constrain the network

**PINNs** (Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis, 2019) put the PDE residual in the loss and used automatic differentiation to evaluate it. The formulation is mesh-free, handles inverse problems naturally, and absorbs sparse data without interpolation — and it is slower than a conventional solver on any forward problem a conventional solver can do.

The more durable contribution of this era was conceptual. **Karniadakis et al. (2021)** organised the whole field around three ways physics can enter a learning problem:

| Bias | How physics enters | Examples on this site |
|---|---|---|
| **Observational** | Training data generated by the physics | [Operators](neural-operators.md), [surrogates](surrogates-rom.md) |
| **Inductive** | Architecture makes the constraint unbreakable | Tensor-basis networks, equivariant GNNs, [relative coordinates](gnn-meshes.md) |
| **Learning** | Penalty terms steer the optimiser toward it | [PINN residual losses](pinns.md), soft conservation |

The ordering matters: an inductive bias is a guarantee, a learning bias is a preference the optimiser may trade away. Most disappointing results in this field come from using the third where the second was available.

### Era 4 — Operators (2019–2023): learn the solution map

The reframing that made the field industrially interesting: learn not \(u = f(x)\) but the map \(\mathcal{G}: a \mapsto u\) from input *function* to solution *function*. A trained operator answers a whole parametric family in one model, at inference cost.

**DeepONet** (Lu, Jin & Karniadakis, 2019/2021) realised this with a branch–trunk decomposition backed by an operator universal-approximation theorem. The **Fourier Neural Operator** (Li, Kovachki, Azizzadenesheli, Anandkumar et al., 2020/2021) made the kernel integral global and cheap by doing it as a multiplication in Fourier space, and demonstrated **discretisation invariance** — train at one resolution, evaluate at another. **Kovachki et al. (2023)** put the family on a common theoretical footing.

Alongside, **MeshGraphNets** (Pfaff et al., 2021) showed that message passing on the simulation mesh learns a stencil that transfers to new geometries and resolutions — the first line of attack on irregular domains.

Era 4's limitation is the one that defined Era 5: FNO wants a regular grid, and real engineering geometry is neither regular nor fixed.

### Era 5 — Geometry at scale (2024–present): learn across shapes

**Transolver** (Wu et al., ICML 2024) introduced *physics attention*: rather than attending over mesh points, learn a set of slices that group points in similar physical states, and attend over those. The cost becomes linear in points, and the method stops caring whether the domain is a grid.

Industrial-scale external aerodynamics followed quickly, and this is where the aerospace relevance is:

- **AB-UPT** (anchored-branched universal physics transformers, 2025) scales to full-vehicle meshes by separating anchor points from branched queries.
- **DoMINO** (NVIDIA, 2025) — *Decomposable Multi-scale Iterative Neural Operator* — works directly from STL point clouds, combining a global multi-scale geometry encoding with a local stencil sampled around each query point, and predicts surface pressure and wall shear alongside volume fields.
- **GeoTransolver** (Adams, Ranade, Cherukuri & Choudhry, 2025/26) adds geometry-aware latent embeddings to the Transolver backbone, cross-attending to a shared multi-scale geometry context at *every* block so geometric information is not lost with depth.

All three are implemented in **[NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo](aerospace-applications.md)**, which is the practical reason they are usable: distributed training, sharded tensors for meshes too large for one GPU, and the data curation needed to turn thousands of CFD runs into a training set.

## Mental model

Read the chronology as a sequence of answers to one question: *what does a single training investment buy you?*

Projection ROMs amortise over **parameters at one geometry**. Operators amortise over a **parametric family**. Geometry-aware transformers amortise over a **class of shapes**. Each step widened the denominator, and each step cost more training data — which is why the field tracked the availability of GPUs and of large curated CFD datasets rather than tracking any mathematical breakthrough.

The second thread is the slow migration of physics from the loss function into the architecture. The field tried soft penalties first because they are easy, found them unreliable, and has been moving constraints into structure ever since — invariant bases, equivariant message passing, divergence-free parameterisations, geometry-aware attention.

## Numerics / practice

- **Date the method before adopting it.** A 2019 PINN paper and a 2025 transformer operator are not competing for the same job, and benchmark comparisons that put them on one axis are usually measuring the wrong thing.
- **Prefer an inductive bias to a learning bias** wherever the constraint can be built in. A penalty term is a hope; an invariant architecture is a guarantee.
- **Ask what the training investment amortises over** before estimating payback. The worked example below shows the answer swings the economics by an order of magnitude.
- **Check which benchmark a claim comes from.** DrivAerML, SHIFT-SUV, SHIFT-Wing and CarBench have different geometry variability, and a model tuned on one does not transfer its reported accuracy to another.

??? warning "Failure modes"
    **Treating the newest method as the best method.** The eras answer different questions. A single forward solve still belongs to a conventional solver; an inverse problem with sparse data still suits a PINN; a 500-sample UQ study at one geometry is still ROM territory. Reaching for a transformer operator because it is current is the most common waste in the field.

    **Soft constraints assumed to hold.** A residual penalty is one term in a weighted sum, and the optimiser will trade it against the data term whenever that lowers total loss. Conservation "enforced" this way is satisfied approximately, at an accuracy nobody measured, and usually worst exactly where the solution is hardest.

    **Benchmark accuracy read as design accuracy.** Reported \(R^2\) is measured against the spread of a test population. If your design deltas are much smaller than that spread — and in aerodynamics they always are — the same model that looks excellent cannot rank your candidates. Quantified on the [aerospace applications](aerospace-applications.md) page.

    **A priori closure performance believed.** The turbulence-modelling line learned this early and the lesson keeps being re-learned: offline correlation with true stresses predicts almost nothing about stability and accuracy in a running solver. See [hybrid coupling](hybrid-coupling.md).

    **Discretisation invariance over-claimed.** FNO is resolution-invariant in the sense that it can be evaluated on a finer grid. That is not the same as being *accurate* there — unresolved scales absent from training do not appear because the evaluation grid got finer.

    **Citing the review instead of the method.** The reviews in Sources below are maps, not primary results. Claims about what a method achieves should be traced to the paper that achieved it, because review summaries compress away the conditions that made the result hold.

## Worked example

What each era's training investment actually buys, in the only unit that matters — high-fidelity solves:

```python
import numpy as np

# All costs in units of ONE high-fidelity CFD solve. Inference is ~1e-5 of a solve: free.
# The question every method below is asked: "the flow over a NEW geometry in this class."
Q      = 500     # flow evaluations wanted per design study
M_rom  = 40      # snapshots to build a projection ROM, valid at ONE geometry
N_fam  = 1000    # training solves for an operator over ONE parametric family
N_geo  = 2500    # training solves for a geometry-generalising operator over the CLASS
TRAIN  = 20      # GPU training, in CFD-solve equivalents

def totals(G):
    """G = number of distinct geometries the campaign must cover."""
    return {"direct CFD":        G*Q,
            "ROM per geometry":  G*(M_rom + Q*0.0),
            "operator per family": G*(N_fam + TRAIN),
            "geometry-aware operator": N_geo + TRAIN}

cols = list(totals(1))
print(f"cost to cover G distinct geometries, {Q} evaluations each (CFD-solve equivalents)")
print(f"{'G':>4}" + "".join(f"{c:>26}" for c in cols))
for G in (1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100):
    t = totals(G)
    print(f"{G:4d}" + "".join(f"{t[c]:26,.0f}" for c in cols))

print("\nbreak-even of the geometry-aware operator against each alternative:")
for c in cols[:-1]:
    G = next((g for g in range(1, 10**6) if totals(g)["geometry-aware operator"] < totals(g)[c]), None)
    print(f"   vs {c:<22} G = {G}")

print("\nbut the cost table alone ranks them wrongly -- what each is VALID over:")
scope = {"direct CFD":              "anything the solver can mesh",
         "ROM per geometry":        "parameters near ONE geometry; fails on advection (n-width)",
         "operator per family":     "the parametric family it was trained on, fixed discretisation",
         "geometry-aware operator": "unseen geometries in the class, arbitrary point clouds"}
for c in cols:
    print(f"   {c:<24} {scope[c]}")
```

The break-even numbers explain the chronology better than any argument about accuracy. Against direct CFD, a geometry-generalising operator pays for itself after about **six** distinct geometries; against retraining a per-family operator each time, after **three**. That is well inside a single aircraft or vehicle programme, which is why industrial money moved here as soon as the architectures could handle raw geometry.

The ROM column is the one to read carefully. It is far the cheapest and it is not a competitor: forty snapshots buys parametric queries *near one geometry*, and nothing at all for a shape the basis has never seen. Ranking these methods on cost alone puts the narrowest one first — the scope table underneath is what makes the comparison honest, and it is the same mistake as judging a bending-constrained wing by its span efficiency.

The assumptions are deliberately round and the conclusion is not sensitive to them: the crossover sits at a handful of geometries for any plausible choice, because the ratio that matters is training-set size over queries-per-study, and both are known to within a factor of two in practice.
