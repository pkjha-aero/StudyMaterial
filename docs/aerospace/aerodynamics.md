# Aerodynamics

<span class="status status-solid">solid</span>
<span class="pillar">pillar 1 &middot; Physical modeling and domain theory</span>

!!! abstract "In one minute"
    - Lift comes from **circulation**: \(L' = \rho U \Gamma\). The Kutta condition is what selects \(\Gamma\) for a given shape and incidence.
    - Thin airfoil theory gives \(c_\ell = 2\pi\alpha\) and the quarter-chord aerodynamic centre — good to within a few percent below stall, useless above it.
    - **Induced drag is the price of finite span**: \(C_{D_i} = C_L^2/(\pi e \mathrm{AR})\). It falls with speed while profile drag rises, which sets minimum-drag speed.
    - **Prandtl–Glauert** corrects for compressibility until it does not — it is singular at \(M=1\) and unusable transonically.
    - Potential-flow methods cannot predict separation or maximum lift. Those need viscous physics.
    - One **small-disturbance equation** organises the whole speed range; what changes with Mach is its *type* — elliptic subsonic, hyperbolic supersonic, nonlinear and mixed transonic.
    - **Elliptical loading is optimal only at fixed span.** Constrain root bending instead and the optimum is bell-shaped, which is a different wing.
    - Vorticity does not *cause* velocity, and the Biot–Savart law is a kinematic identity rather than a mechanism. Most popular explanations of lift fail on exactly this point.

## Key results

### Lift in 2D

**Kutta–Joukowski**, and thin airfoil theory for a cambered section:

<div class="result" markdown>

\[
L' = \rho U_\infty \Gamma,
\qquad
c_\ell = 2\pi(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}),
\qquad
x_{ac} = \frac{c}{4}
\]

</div>

The lift-curve slope \(2\pi\) per radian (\(\approx 0.11\) per degree) is the reference every real airfoil is measured against; real sections reach 90–95% of it.

### Finite wings

**Prandtl lifting line:**

\[
C_L = \frac{C_{L_\alpha,\,2\mathrm{D}}}{1 + \frac{C_{L_\alpha,\,2\mathrm{D}}}{\pi e \mathrm{AR}}}\,\alpha,
\qquad
C_{D_i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e\, \mathrm{AR}},
\qquad
\alpha_i = \frac{C_L}{\pi e\,\mathrm{AR}}
\]

Elliptical loading gives \(e = 1\) and minimum induced drag; typical wings reach \(e \approx 0.7\text{–}0.85\).

### One equation, three regimes

Linearise the full potential equation about a uniform stream for a thin body, and everything from low subsonic to supersonic comes from one equation:

<div class="result" markdown>

\[
(1 - M_\infty^2)\,\phi_{xx} + \phi_{yy} = 0
\]

</div>

What changes with Mach number is not the equation but its **type**:

| Regime | \(1-M_\infty^2\) | Type | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subsonic | \(> 0\) | **Elliptic** | Disturbances felt everywhere; smooth fields; boundary-value problem |
| Sonic | \(\to 0\) | Degenerate | The linearisation itself fails |
| Supersonic | \(< 0\) | **Hyperbolic** | Disturbances confined to Mach cones; characteristics; waves |

Both classical results are this one equation. **Prandtl–Glauert** is the elliptic branch, rescaling an incompressible solution by \(1/\sqrt{1-M_\infty^2}\). **Ackeret's linearised supersonic theory** is the hyperbolic branch, which for a thin surface at local slope \(\theta\) gives

\[
c_p = \frac{2\theta}{\sqrt{M_\infty^2 - 1}}
\]

and with it wave drag, a drag component that simply does not exist in the elliptic regime. Both diverge at \(M_\infty = 1\), and they diverge because the linearisation drops the term that matters there. Restoring it gives the **transonic small-disturbance equation**, whose leading coefficient depends on the solution itself — the subject of [transonic aerodynamics](transonic.md).

### Drag decomposition

| Component | Scales as | Dominant when |
|---|---|---|
| Induced | \(C_L^2/\mathrm{AR}\), so \(\propto 1/V^2\) | slow, heavy, high \(C_L\) |
| Profile (skin friction + form) | \(\propto V^2\) | fast, low \(C_L\) |
| Wave | onset above \(M_{crit}\) | transonic and above |

Minimum drag occurs where induced equals profile drag — which is also where \(L/D\) is maximum.

### Force accounting: near field and far field

There are two ways to compute the force on a body, and they are not equally useful.

**Near-field** integration sums pressure and shear over the surface. It is direct, and it is numerically awful for drag: drag is a small difference between large opposing contributions, so a 1% error in surface pressure can be a 50% error in drag.

**Far-field** accounting applies momentum and energy balances on a control volume far downstream — the **Trefftz plane**. It is both more accurate and more informative, because the contributions separate physically:

- **Induced drag** appears as kinetic energy left in the cross-flow of the trailing vortex sheet.
- **Profile drag** appears as the momentum defect in the viscous wake.
- **Wave drag** appears as entropy generated by shocks.

A surface integral gives one number; a Trefftz-plane analysis tells you which mechanism to attack. This is why drag decomposition is done in the far field in practice, and why induced drag is best understood as *energy left in the wake* rather than as a force on the wing.

### Minimum induced drag depends on what you hold fixed

For a planar wake at **fixed span**, elliptical loading is optimal and \(e = 1\). That result is quoted so often that the condition attached to it gets dropped, and the condition is the whole content.

Real wings are not span-limited; they are **structure-limited**, and root bending moment is what sets wing weight. Minimising induced drag at fixed lift and fixed *root bending moment* instead gives a different answer: a longer wing carrying a more inboard-loaded, bell-shaped distribution. It is worse by the fixed-span metric — its span efficiency is well below 1 — and better in the only sense that matters. The worked example below computes it.

### The drag polar

\[
C_D = C_{D_0} + \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e \mathrm{AR}},
\qquad
\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi e \mathrm{AR}}{C_{D_0}}}
\]

### Compressibility

Prandtl–Glauert for subsonic flow:

\[
c_p = \frac{c_{p,0}}{\sqrt{1-M_\infty^2}}
\]

Critical Mach \(M_{crit}\) is where local flow first reaches sonic; drag divergence follows shortly after. Sweep delays both by acting on the normal component: \(M_{\text{eff}} \approx M_\infty\cos\Lambda\).

### How stall actually works

Potential flow has no stall mechanism, but the route to stall is not mysterious. The boundary layer has a **displacement effect**: it pushes the outer inviscid flow away from the surface by \(\delta^*\), so the flow sees a slightly different, thicker body. As incidence rises, the upper-surface boundary layer thickens most near the trailing edge, and the effective shape it presents is one with *less camber* than the metal.

This is **viscous decambering**, and it explains what is actually observed: the lift curve bends over gradually before \(C_{L,\max}\) rather than following \(2\pi\alpha\) up to a cliff. The practical consequence is that predicting maximum lift requires a viscous–inviscid coupling, not a correction factor — which is exactly what XFOIL and MSES do, iterating an outer panel solution against an integral boundary layer until the displacement surface is consistent.

That coupling needs care near separation. Driving the boundary layer from a prescribed pressure distribution (**direct** mode) becomes singular at separation; **inverse** and **semi-inverse** schemes, which prescribe displacement thickness or solve both simultaneously, are what make the calculation go through the stall.

### Reynolds effects

**Reynolds effects.** \(C_{L,\max}\) and stall behaviour depend strongly on \(\mathrm{Re}\) — transition location changes, and a laminar separation bubble at low \(\mathrm{Re}\) can halve maximum lift. Wind-tunnel data at \(\mathrm{Re}=10^6\) does not transfer to flight at \(10^7\).

## Mental model

A wing deflects air downwards; the reaction is lift. The downwash it generates at its own location tilts the local flow, so the effective incidence is less than the geometric one, and the lift vector tilts backwards by that angle — that backward component *is* induced drag. Nothing is lost to friction; the energy goes into the trailing vortex system.

That picture explains why span matters more than area for induced drag, why gliders have enormous aspect ratio, and why formation flight saves fuel.

**But be careful with the word "induced".** The Biot–Savart law is a kinematic identity relating a vorticity field to a velocity field that is consistent with it — not a statement that vorticity *produces* velocity, and not a causal mechanism. A vortex does not reach out and push the air around it. Treating the mathematics as physics is the single most common conceptual error in aerodynamics, and it produces confident, wrong intuitions about wakes, wingtip devices and formation flight.

The deeper point is about cause and effect. Lift is not a chain — shape causes velocity causes pressure causes lift — but a **mutual interaction**: the pressure and velocity fields satisfy each other everywhere at once, and the airfoil is a boundary condition on that whole field. This is why every short explanation of lift that tries to name a first cause is unsatisfying:

- **Equal transit time** ("the upper particle must catch up") is simply false — the upper flow arrives sooner — and even if the premise held it would predict far too little lift.
- **Venturi / "the upper surface is a nozzle"** borrows a constriction that is not there, and cannot explain a cambered flat plate or inverted flight.
- **Pure Newtonian deflection** gets the direction and the momentum budget right but says nothing about why the flow follows a convex upper surface at all, which is the actual question.

A satisfactory account needs the reciprocal relation between streamline curvature and the pressure gradient normal to it, applied to the whole field: the flow curves because there is a pressure gradient, and the pressure gradient exists because the flow curves. Those are the same fact, which is precisely why no one-way story works.

## Numerics / practice

- **Panel methods** (vortex/source/doublet) solve potential flow cheaply and are excellent for attached, thin-boundary-layer cases. Coupled to an integral boundary-layer method (as XFOIL does) they predict separation onset reasonably.
- **Report \(\mathrm{Re}\) and \(M\)** with any aerodynamic coefficient. Without both, the number is not interpretable.
- **Check \(e\)** against the planform rather than assuming 1. Taper, twist and fuselage interference all reduce it.
- **Compute drag in the far field, not by surface integration**, whenever you can. Near-field drag is a small difference of large numbers; the Trefftz plane also tells you which mechanism it came from.
- **State the constraint before quoting an optimum spanload.** "Elliptical is best" is true at fixed span and misleading at fixed root bending moment.
- **Use inverse or semi-inverse coupling near separation.** Direct viscous–inviscid iteration is singular there and will stall or diverge just where the answer matters.
- For CFD, see [fluids › CFD](../fluids/cfd/index.md); the failure modes there apply directly.

??? warning "Failure modes"
    **Thin airfoil theory near stall.** \(c_\ell = 2\pi\alpha\) has no maximum — extrapolate it past about 10–12° and it predicts lift a real wing cannot produce. It is a linear theory with no viscous physics.

    **Prandtl–Glauert approaching \(M=1\).** The \(1/\sqrt{1-M^2}\) factor diverges. Above roughly \(M=0.7\) it is already poor and transonically it is meaningless. Symptom: pressure coefficients growing without bound as the design Mach rises.

    **Lifting line at low aspect ratio.** The theory assumes high \(\mathrm{AR}\) and small sweep. Below \(\mathrm{AR}\approx4\), or on strongly swept or delta wings, it is wrong in kind — those wings generate lift partly through leading-edge vortices, a mechanism the theory does not contain.

    **2D section data applied directly to a 3D wing.** Ignores downwash, so it overpredicts lift-curve slope and omits induced drag entirely. The correction is not small: at \(\mathrm{AR}=8\) the 3D slope is about 80% of 2D.

    **Reynolds number mismatch.** Sub-scale tunnel data at \(\mathrm{Re}\sim10^5\)–\(10^6\) can show laminar separation bubbles absent at flight \(\mathrm{Re}\). \(C_{L,\max}\) is the most affected quantity and the one most often transferred uncorrected.

    **Potential-flow methods asked to predict separation.** They will return an answer — a smooth, attached, plausible one — at 25° incidence. There is no mechanism in the formulation for the flow to leave the surface.

    **"Elliptical loading is optimal" quoted without its constraint.** It is optimal at *fixed span*. Wings are limited by structure, not span, and under a root-bending constraint the optimum is a longer, bell-shaped load with span efficiency well below 1. Judging that wing by \(e\) marks the better design as the worse one.

    **Biot-Savart read as a causal mechanism.** Vorticity does not induce velocity in any physical sense; the relation is kinematic. Intuitions built on vortices "pushing" air give wrong answers about wake behaviour, wingtip devices and ground effect.

    **Drag computed by surface integration.** It is a small difference between large pressure contributions, so modest local errors become large drag errors, and the answer carries no decomposition. Far-field accounting is both more accurate and more diagnostic.

    **Linear theory used near \(M = 1\) from either side.** Prandtl-Glauert and Ackeret are the elliptic and hyperbolic branches of the *same* linearised equation, and both are singular at \(M_\infty = 1\). Approaching sonic conditions from the supersonic side is no safer than from the subsonic side.

    **Direct viscous-inviscid coupling pushed to separation.** The direct problem is singular at the separation point. A solver run in direct mode near \(C_{L,\max}\) will fail to converge or converge to nonsense, and the fix is an inverse or semi-inverse formulation rather than more iterations.

    **Ignoring trim drag.** The tail download to trim reduces effective lift and adds drag. Cruise polars built from wing-alone data are optimistic.

## Worked example

### What "optimal" spanload means depends on the constraint

Elliptical loading minimises induced drag at fixed span. Hold the *root bending moment* fixed instead — the quantity that actually sets wing weight — and let span float:

```python
import numpy as np

# Lifting line, symmetric load: Gamma ~ sum_{n odd} A_n sin(n theta),  y = -(b/2) cos theta
#   lift ~ b^2 A_1     induced drag ~ b^2 sum n A_n^2     root bending ~ b^3 sum A_n I_n
NS = np.array([1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13])
th = np.linspace(1e-12, np.pi - 1e-12, 400001); dth = th[1] - th[0]
m  = th >= np.pi/2                                   # outboard half-span
I  = np.array([np.sum(np.sin(n*th[m])*(-np.cos(th[m]))*np.sin(th[m]))*dth for n in NS])
Q  = np.diag(NS.astype(float))

def optimum(s):
    """Min induced drag at span ratio s, holding lift and root bending at elliptic values."""
    C   = np.vstack([np.eye(len(NS))[0]*s**2, I*s**3])     # rows: lift, root bending
    K   = np.block([[2*s**2*Q, C.T], [C, np.zeros((2, 2))]])
    rhs = np.concatenate([np.zeros(len(NS)), [1.0, I[0]]])  # elliptic baseline: b=1, A1=1
    A   = np.linalg.solve(K, rhs)[:len(NS)]
    return A, s**2*np.sum(NS*A**2)

print(f"I_1 = {I[0]:.5f}  (exactly 1/3)      elliptic baseline: Di = {optimum(1.0)[1]:.3f}")
print("\nminimum induced drag at fixed lift AND fixed root bending moment")
print(f"{'b/b1':>6}{'Di/Di1':>9}{'elliptic Di':>13}{'span eff e':>12}{'A3/A1':>9}")
for s in (1.0, 1.05, 1.1, 1.2, 1.25, 1.3, 1.4):
    A, Di = optimum(s)
    print(f"{s:6.2f}{Di:9.3f}{s**-2:13.3f}{A[0]**2/np.sum(NS*A**2):12.3f}{A[1]/A[0]:9.3f}")

print("\nPrandtl's 1933 bell-shaped load, Gamma ~ (1-eta^2)^(3/2), is A3/A1 = -1/3 exactly:")
eta  = -np.cos(th)
bell = (1 - eta**2)**1.5
Ab   = np.array([2/np.pi*np.sum(bell*np.sin(n*th))*dth for n in NS])
print(f"   sine-series of the bell : A3/A1 = {Ab[1]/Ab[0]:+.4f}, A5/A1 = {Ab[2]/Ab[0]:+.4f}")
s_bell = min(np.arange(1.0, 1.5, 1e-4), key=lambda s: abs(optimum(s)[0][1]/optimum(s)[0][0] + 1/3))
print(f"   reached at b/b1 = {s_bell:.3f}, where Di/Di1 = {optimum(s_bell)[1]:.3f}")
```

```
   reached at b/b1 = 1.263, where Di/Di1 = 0.844
```

Three things in that table are worth separating.

The elliptic column is what you get by stretching an elliptically loaded wing: drag falls as \(b^{-2}\), fast and without limit, which is why span is the most powerful induced-drag lever there is. The constrained column is what you can actually build: the same stretch is available only if the load is pulled inboard to keep the root bending moment fixed, and the benefit flattens out near **0.84** by about \(b/b_1 = 1.3\). Past that, more span buys almost nothing.

The span-efficiency column is the trap. At the optimum, \(e \approx 0.70\) — by the fixed-span figure of merit this wing looks 30% worse than elliptical, while actually producing 16% less drag for the same lift and the same structure. Quoting \(e\) for a bending-constrained design ranks it backwards.

And the load that emerges is not a new invention: the optimum passes through \(A_3/A_1 = -1/3\) exactly, which is Prandtl's 1933 bell-shaped distribution \(\Gamma \sim (1-\eta^2)^{3/2}\), at about 26% more span. The computation above reproduces Drela's published figures for this problem — 0.881 against his 0.882 at \(b/b_1 = 1.10\), and 0.844 against his 0.845 at 1.30.

One caveat the numbers themselves show: beyond \(b/b_1 \approx 1.3\) the idealised problem keeps improving slightly, because only root bending is constrained and nothing penalises the increasingly negative outboard loading. Real designs stop here for reasons — torsion, tip stall, flutter, span limits at the gate — that this model does not contain.

### Where minimum drag sits, and why it is also best \(L/D\):

```python
import numpy as np

CD0, e, AR = 0.022, 0.80, 9.0
k  = 1.0 / (np.pi * e * AR)
CL = np.sqrt(CD0 / k)                    # induced drag == profile drag
LD = CL / (CD0 + k * CL**2)
print(f"k            = {k:.5f}")
print(f"CL at min drag = {CL:.3f}")
print(f"(L/D)max       = {LD:.2f}")
print(f"check formula  = {0.5*np.sqrt(np.pi*e*AR/CD0):.2f}")
```

```
k            = 0.04421
CL at min drag = 0.705
(L/D)max       = 16.03
check formula  = 16.03
```

Best \(L/D\) occurs at exactly the \(C_L\) where the two drag contributions are equal — which is why a clean, high-aspect-ratio wing flies its best-range speed slower than intuition suggests.
